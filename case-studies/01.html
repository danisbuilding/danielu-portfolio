Pulling the plug on an AI feature for a marketplace's power users.

A global services marketplace · Senior UX Researcher (AI Product)
Outcome
Redirected the AI roadmap from a single open-ended assistant to a guided, novice-first design. Protected the workflows of revenue-critical power users.
Methods
Prototype testing · Behavioral walkthroughs · Segment analysis · Mental-model probing
Role
Senior UX Researcher embedded with the AI product team. Owned the research strategy, ran the studies, and presented the final recommendation directly to product leadership.

CONTEXT

The product team had a strong hypothesis: an AI assistant inside the job-post creation flow would help clients write stronger descriptions, leading to faster hiring, better matches, and revenue lift. Job-post creation is a high-leverage moment in the marketplace lifecycle, so the upside was real if the assumption held.

PROBLEM

Three assumptions sat under the proposal: clients struggle to write good descriptions, AI reduces friction, and open-ended prompting feels empowering. None had been tested against actual client behavior. The deeper strategic question was whether AI should enhance, guide, or replace part of a mature, revenue-critical workflow. The wrong answer would be expensive in either direction.

APPROACH

I designed evaluative research targeting two segments that mattered for the decision: high-spending repeat enterprise clients, who drive disproportionate revenue, and newer, less-experienced clients, who carry higher posting uncertainty. The work used prototype testing, observed workflow simulations, behavioral walkthroughs of post creation, and probing around AI mental models. The goal was to separate "this feels new" reactions from actual behavioral change.

WHAT WE LEARNED

For newer clients, AI help reduced cognitive load. They welcomed structure, used suggestions productively, and accepted help translating vague needs into structured posts. The hypothesis held for them.

For power users, the picture inverted. High-performing clients had already optimized: refined internal templates, deep understanding of marketplace mechanics, efficient posting workflows. AI felt unnecessary, disrupted rather than streamlined, and introduced ambiguity into a well-tuned process. Since this segment generated outsized revenue, the disruption was costly.

The system told experienced clients how to do something they had already gotten good at, and got in the way of the workflow that was earning the platform money.

The open-ended prompt model, "type what you need, let AI figure it out," also created friction across both segments. Users were unclear how to prompt effectively, the system lacked guardrails, and the interface did not scaffold expectations well enough for novices to succeed without trial and error.

RECOMMENDATION

I recommended sunsetting the open-ended assistant in its proposed form and pivoting to a guided, novice-first design: templates as anchors, scaffolded prompts, expectations clearly framed. Power users would not be touched. Novices would get the help they actually needed.

The team accepted the recommendation. The AI roadmap was reset around the novice-first frame.
